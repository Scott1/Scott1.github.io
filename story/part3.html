Part 3: Surrounded by Experts, Afraid to Speak, and Learning in Silence

When I left Symantec for Immunet, I thought I was making a move toward something more modern, more exciting, and more meaningful.

I didn’t have a perfectly clear story for it at the time, but I could feel the appeal. Symantec had shown me that structure alone didn’t make great software. The startup world, at least from the outside, seemed like the opposite of that kind of stagnation. It promised speed, innovation, and people who were genuinely trying to build something new instead of just maintaining large, aging systems.

That mattered to me.

So when the opportunity came up, I took it.

And almost immediately, I realized I had stepped into a completely different world.

A Different Kind of Team

The first thing that struck me at Immunet was the team itself.

At TELUS, I had worked with people who were trying to get results quickly, often without much engineering discipline. At Symantec, I had worked with people who were capable, but often siloed, fragmented, and not especially interested in pulling a junior person into the deeper layers of how things worked.

At Immunet, the feeling was entirely different.

This was the first time in my career where I was surrounded by people I would now confidently describe as real experts.

They weren’t just confident.
They weren’t just good presenters.
They weren’t just occupying roles with authority.

They actually knew what they were doing.

You could see it in how they talked to each other. You could see it in the speed of their thinking, in the assumptions they challenged, in the way they could take a messy technical problem and start shaping it into something concrete through conversation alone. There was a shared technical fluency in the room that I hadn’t experienced before.

When several of them got into a discussion, it was impressive to watch.

They would disagree openly, test ideas against each other, throw out alternatives, refine things in real time, and somehow move from uncertainty to a working direction incredibly quickly. The pace of those conversations could be hard to follow, but the quality of the thinking was obvious.

For the first time, I felt like I had found what I had been looking for since leaving TELUS.

Experts.

Real ones.

And that should have been exactly what I wanted.

In some ways, it was.

But it also triggered something in me almost immediately.

A Completely New Technical World

Up until that point, my technical world had been fairly consistent.

I was a Windows developer. I had worked in C# and C++. I was used to developing on Windows, for software that ran on Windows, with tools and environments that mostly lived comfortably in that ecosystem.

That was the world I knew.

Immunet was not that world.

The culture of the team was rooted much more in Linux, Unix, and macOS. Most of the people there were deeply comfortable in that environment, and there was a kind of unspoken contempt for Windows that I found both amusing and intimidating. I still needed a Windows machine for my actual development work because the client we were building ran on Windows, and there was really no substitute for building and testing it there. But almost everything else around me lived in a different ecosystem.

The repositories, the server-side systems, the infrastructure, the command-line tooling, the broader environment in which the product lived and breathed — all of that was outside what I was used to.

So even before I got into the product itself, I had to start learning a new way of operating.

I had to become more comfortable with:

SSH
SCP
working directly from the command line
shell tools like grep, sed, and awk
scripting in environments that felt unfamiliar and less forgiving than what I had been using before

That alone was a significant shift.

But layered on top of that was the actual product.

And the product was unlike anything I had worked on before.

This Was the First Time Security Was Really the Work

At TELUS, security had barely entered the picture. We weren’t building security products, and there was almost no thought given to how the software we wrote might itself become a vulnerability, or how it might be exploited, or what it meant to build something resilient in the presence of malicious intent. That simply wasn’t the world we were in.

At Symantec, I had worked for a security company, but not really in the heart of its security work. The product I was on — SPN — was more of an extension, an add-on, an attempt to move into adjacent spaces like remote backup and remote access. I wasn’t building malware detection systems. I wasn’t dealing directly with the guts of antivirus. And surprisingly, at least from my perspective, there still wasn’t a particularly deep or constant focus on how the code we were writing might itself be exploited. Security was the broader brand, but not yet the daily technical reality of my role.

Immunet changed that completely.

This was the first time I was truly working inside a product whose purpose was advanced malware protection.

Now the product wasn’t just software. It was security software. Its reason for existing was to detect threats, stop them, report on them, and protect machines in real time.

That meant I had to learn a whole new domain.

Not just technically, but conceptually. There was a language around security work that I had never really lived inside before:

  • detections
  • false positives
  • false negatives
  • file dispositions
  • signatures
  • detection engines
  • heuristics
  • telemetry
  • malware analysis
  • event reporting

This wasn’t abstract knowledge. It was built into the work itself.

I was now working around systems that:

detected malware
reported on system activity
surfaced detections to users
managed the communication between endpoint software and cloud systems that informed those decisions

And because the team was small and highly capable, there was a feeling that everyone should know the whole product. Not just their narrow area, but the broader system, its logic, its moving parts, and the security ideas behind it.

In a large company, a product of that size and complexity could easily be spread across hundreds of people in many silos. But here, a handful of experts knew almost everything.

That was amazing to see.

And deeply intimidating.

Learning Without Ever Admitting How Much I Didn’t Know

This was the part I didn’t handle well.

I was surrounded by people who clearly had deep expertise — not just in software, but in security specifically. Most of them had come from security backgrounds. They had been thinking in this domain for years. The language was natural to them. The priorities were natural to them. The trade-offs were natural to them.

I had not come from that world.

And instead of leaning into that and saying, “I don’t know this yet — can you help me understand it?” I did what had become natural to me by then.

I stayed quiet.

I watched.

I listened.

I tried to piece things together on my own.

Part of that was my own fear. I didn’t want to expose how far behind I felt. I didn’t want to confirm the suspicion I always carried that I was somehow less legitimate, less technical, less expert than the people around me.

But part of it was also the environment.

The pace was relentless. Everything moved quickly. There was always more work to do, more features to build, more fires to put out, more problems to solve. It didn’t feel like the kind of culture where someone would say, “Let’s slow down and bring the junior person fully up to speed on the whole system.” No one explicitly said that learning was unwelcome. But the pressure of the environment made it feel like something you were expected to do on your own time, in your own head, while still keeping up with the work.

So I did.

Or at least I tried to.

The Work Itself

My role was still focused on the client side of the product.

That meant I was responsible for:

  • the installer
  • the GUI
  • the build process
  • and some of the client-side systems that tied all of that together

The endpoint side of the product itself was substantial. It wasn’t just “an app” running on Windows. It included:

an installer to get everything onto the machine properly
a GUI process that users interacted with
an agent process acting as the brains of the client
system-level drivers monitoring file and process activity
local storage and supporting services
communication with cloud systems that provided updated malware intelligence and other functionality

There were also pieces of the client side that reached into cloud-based systems for updates, reporting, crash information, event handling, and all sorts of supporting interactions. So even though I wasn’t writing the deepest detection logic myself, I was constantly working around the infrastructure that made detection visible, usable, and actionable.

And unlike earlier jobs, the constraints were much sharper.

Two things mattered enormously in security software:

  • performance
  • safety

An antivirus product could not be seen as dragging a machine down. It couldn’t be the thing hogging CPU, slowing down a user’s computer, or becoming the reason people hated having it installed. And it absolutely could not become a vulnerability itself — software intended to protect the machine could not become the way a machine was compromised.

Those priorities changed the way the work felt.

Efficiency mattered more. Security hygiene mattered more. Reliability mattered more. The cost of mistakes felt higher.

And everyone around me seemed to understand that intuitively.

I was still learning it.

The First Time I Saw Group Chat as Real Work

One of the more surprising differences at Immunet was how communication worked.

At my earlier jobs, chat existed, but it was mostly used sparingly. One-to-one questions. Quick clarifications. Email still carried much of the weight of serious communication.

At Immunet, group chat was a living part of the work.

It was always active.

And it wasn’t noise.

That was the part that fascinated me.

It wasn’t people wasting time, joking around endlessly, or talking in half-formed fragments. The chats were often extremely productive. People would ask pointed questions, propose ideas, reply quickly, challenge assumptions, and move discussions forward in surprisingly concise, high-quality ways. The communication was raw, direct, and effective. There wasn’t much fluff.

I remember being amazed that these same people who were constantly active in chat were also getting so much work done. It felt like another part of the technical culture I was stepping into — another sign that this team operated at a level I hadn’t seen before.

Even when I wasn’t contributing much, I was learning from those chats.

I was seeing how experts thought in real time.

A Front Row Seat to How Great Teams Work

One of the most valuable parts of that period was that the team was small enough that almost everyone was exposed to almost everything.

We all worked in the same office. We all sat near each other. Almost every conversation was visible. Almost every technical disagreement was audible. Almost every design debate, every trade-off discussion, every urgent problem-solving session happened within range.

That meant I had a front row seat to something I had not really seen before:

what it looks like when a group of genuinely capable, aligned people solve hard problems together.

That mattered.

Because it showed me that expertise wasn’t just theoretical. It wasn’t just something people claimed. It was visible in how they worked. The way they converged on solutions. The way they held a complicated system in their heads and in their shared conversation. The way they could move from messy reality to working software in a remarkably short period of time.

It also taught me that a small, aligned, high-skill team could build something that in another organization might have required hundreds of people.

That realization would stay with me for a long time.

Startup Culture at Its Best

Another thing that made that period special was how close we were to the work itself.

The VP who presented the product to customers, took calls, talked to sales, and understood the competitive landscape sat just a desk or two away from me. The product wasn’t being filtered through five levels of management or through an abstract product process. We knew what customers were asking for. We knew what problems mattered. We knew why certain features were urgent.

That kind of proximity does something important.

It makes the work feel real.

When you understand why a feature matters, who it matters to, and what problem it is solving, the work has energy. It has context. It has a point.

And because we were small, everyone was involved.

Not equally, not in exactly the same way, but there was a shared sense that:

  • everyone’s voice mattered
  • everyone was responsible to each other
  • everyone was close enough to the work to understand why it mattered

There wasn’t much bureaucracy. There wasn’t much ladder-climbing. It didn’t feel like some people were there to “manage” while others were there to merely execute. It felt like a real team.

That feeling would become very important later, because once it started to disappear, I would realize how much I had been relying on it.

The Pressure of Not Fitting In

For all of the growth and learning, one thing did not improve.

I still didn’t feel like I fit.

Externally, I was doing fine. I was contributing. I was learning. I was delivering. But internally, I felt completely outmatched.

At Symantec, I had thought everyone was better than me. At Immunet, that feeling multiplied. These weren’t just competent developers in a big company. These were genuinely impressive people with deep expertise, strong opinions, fast minds, and obvious passion for the domain.

And there was another difference too.

They didn’t just seem better than me.

They seemed more naturally aligned with the work.

They wanted to be there. They seemed to love it. They could spend absurd hours immersed in security software and still feel energized by it. There was a level of identification with the work that I didn’t feel.

I noticed that. And I was ashamed of it.

I remember hearing someone once described as a “lifestyle guy,” meaning someone who cared too much about life outside work, family, friends, balance — as if that made him less committed, less serious, less fit for a team like this.

That landed hard for me.

Because I knew, immediately, that I was a lifestyle guy too.

I just didn’t say it out loud.

So in addition to trying to hide how much I felt behind technically, I was also trying to hide the fact that I did not want my whole life to be work.

That created a deeper sense of not belonging.

Finding Meditation

Around this same time, I started searching for something.

At the time, I probably wouldn’t have described it that way. It felt more like restlessness, confusion, and quiet panic. I was looking up things like:

  • what makes a good software engineer
  • am I a bad developer
  • am I cut out for this kind of work
  • how do you find meaning in your work

Somehow, through that search, I came across the writing of Aaron Swartz. I don’t remember exactly how. I just remember that his writing felt thoughtful, reflective, and serious in a way that cut through the usual noise.

In one of his posts, he talked about things that had made a meaningful difference in his life.

One of them was mindfulness meditation.

I had never heard those words together before.

I didn’t know what it meant. But something about it caught me. So I started reading. Researching. Looking into what it was.

Eventually, after enough hesitation, I tried it.

It felt strange.

Awkward.

Pointless, sometimes.

I didn’t feel good at it.

I didn’t feel like it gave me immediate benefits.

And during my time at Immunet, I don’t think it changed much on the surface. The work still felt overwhelming. I still felt out of place. I still felt like I wasn’t good enough.

But looking back, it mattered enormously.

Because it was one of the first times in my adult life that I responded to a deeper internal signal — a feeling that there had to be more to life than just trying to survive the next role, the next team, the next environment.

At the time, I didn’t know what that meant.

I just knew something in me was looking for another way to make sense of things.

What I Took With Me

That period at Immunet changed me more than I understood at the time.

It taught me how much I could grow when pushed into an environment that demanded more than I thought I could give. It stretched me technically, exposing me to security, Linux/Unix systems, command-line tooling, client-server communication, and a much more sophisticated product environment than anything I had worked on before.

It also showed me what true expertise looked like.

Experts were real. They existed.

But I was too afraid to fully learn from them.

And that distinction mattered.

Because it also showed me that expertise alone doesn’t guarantee mentorship, leadership, or the ability to bring others along. A team can be brilliant and still not know how to make itself a safe place for a more junior person to learn in public.

At the same time, I got my first taste of startup culture at its best: closeness to the product, closeness to users, speed without total chaos, and a real sense that what we were building mattered.

That feeling would become one of the most important reference points in my career.

Because later, when it started to disappear, I would finally understand how much I had been relying on it.

And underneath all of that, quietly, meditation had entered my life.

It wasn’t changing me dramatically yet.

But something had started.

A different way of paying attention.

A different way of relating to my mind.

A small crack in the certainty of the world I had been living in.

I didn’t know it then, but that crack would eventually become one of the most important parts of the story.
