Part 5: Startup on Steroids, Architecture as Clarity, and the First Real Shift in How I Saw Myself

When Cisco acquired Sourcefire, I expected a clean break. I assumed the startup chapter would finally be swallowed by the larger enterprise.

At first, though, that’s not what happened. The early period after the Cisco acquisition felt surprisingly good. It felt less like we had been absorbed and more like we had been given a much bigger engine.

The Culture Was Still Alive

The first phase after the Cisco acquisition still felt energized. We were still close to the product and the reasons the product mattered.

There were more meetings and more structure forming, but it still felt like one team. A bigger team, yes. A more visible team. But still a team.

The First Architecture Moment

I was working with a newer developer on a feature that touched multiple parts of the system. Instead of jumping straight into code, I started sketching out the system.

Nothing fancy. A simple architecture diagram: boxes, arrows, labels, enough detail to explain boundaries and interactions.

We walked through it together before he started coding, and the effect was immediate. The feature came together faster, with less confusion and less rework.

The Second Architecture Moment

There was another meeting involving development and QA where we were trying to reason through a problem. People were talking in abstractions. The room was drifting toward confusion rather than clarity.

So I drew a picture. A rough architecture diagram. A few boxes. A simple sequence of how the pieces related to each other.

Almost immediately the temperature in the room changed. What had been abstract became concrete. People could point to the same thing and say, yes, this is the problem.

Meditation Becomes More Than an Idea

My meditation practice started to deepen. It didn’t make me instantly calm or wise, but every once in a while, there would be a glimpse.

Moments where the usual internal noise softened. Moments where my sense of self — anxious, comparing, striving, defending — seemed less solid.

The VP Story

There was one person who crystallized this shift for me: a VP who had always intimidated me. He was passionate, physically imposing, deeply knowledgeable, direct, and charismatic.

Then one day, after my meditation practice had deepened, I saw him walk into the room and instead of tightening, I felt calm. I remember thinking: he’s just another person.

That moment did not erase years of conditioning, but it showed me that my relationship to these experts could change.

Learning Versus Knowing

This period taught me the difference between learning something and knowing something. Learning is conceptual. Knowing is lived.

I could read about ego, mindfulness, and non-reactivity, but none of it landed the same way until I experienced it directly.

What This Period Changed

Architecture started to feel real to me: not as a grand formal discipline, but as a practical way of creating clarity and shared understanding.

It also softened the grip of imposter syndrome. For the first time, it started to feel possible that the story I had been telling myself about who I was was not the only story available.
