Part 1: Quick Wins, Cowboy Coding, and the Beginning of Doubt

I didn’t set out to build a career in software.

I didn’t have a plan.
I didn’t have a clear direction.
I mostly just needed a job.

That job came through TELUS.

My dad worked there, and like a lot of people starting out, that connection helped me get my foot in the door. My interview lasted maybe twenty minutes. I don’t remember being particularly impressive—I just didn’t say anything that disqualified me.

That was enough.

The Job That Didn’t Feel Like a Career

I started in Local Number Portability at a time when telecom competition was just opening up.

On paper, it sounded technical.

In reality, it was repetitive, manual work.

A typical task meant:

  • opening one system
  • copying information
  • pasting it into another
  • repeating that process across 15–20 different tools

Over and over. All day.

It didn’t require much thinking once you understood the process.

It was tedious. Predictable. And honestly, kind of numbing.

But it paid well—much better than most jobs my friends had—and it allowed me to put myself through university without taking on debt.

So I stayed.

The Moment Things Changed (Quietly)

Near the end of my computer science degree, something shifted.

Not dramatically.

Just enough to notice.

I stopped seeing the work as individual tasks and started seeing the pattern behind them.

Every order followed the same structure.
The same inputs.
The same outputs.
The same sequence of steps.

And the tools we were using—mostly terminal-based systems—had just enough flexibility to support scripting.

So I started experimenting.

At first, it was small things:

  • automating a few keystrokes
  • reducing repetitive clicks

Nothing fancy.

But once you start down that path, it’s hard to stop.

“Why Am I Even Doing This Manually?”

The small optimizations turned into larger ones.

Then entire workflows.

Then eventually, almost everything I was doing manually became automated.

I didn’t think of it as anything special at the time. It just felt like the obvious thing to do.

But the impact was… noticeable.

My output went from normal to absurd.

I was completing work at a rate that didn’t make sense compared to the rest of the team—something like 100x more than the top performers.

At first, my manager assumed the reporting system was broken.

When I explained what I had built, I expected to get in trouble.

Instead, she told me to keep going.

That moment mattered more than I realized.

It was the first time I thought:

Maybe I actually know what I’m doing.

The Quick Wins Team

Not long after, I was introduced to a small internal team.

They were working on the same problem I had stumbled into—automating inefficient processes across the company.

Their name said everything: Quick Wins.

Their approach was simple:

  • find something slow
  • automate it
  • move on

No long planning cycles.
No large system redesigns.
Just results.

I joined the team.

And for the first time, the work felt exciting.

Speed Over Everything

We worked directly with frontline employees, watching how they did their jobs and looking for opportunities to improve things.

When we found something, we built a solution.

Fast.

We didn’t wait for perfect designs.
We didn’t follow structured processes.
We didn’t have QA cycles.

Sometimes testing happened directly in production.

Looking back, it was the definition of what I would later learn is called:

cowboy coding

At the time, it just felt like momentum.

The Part That Didn’t Feel Right

There was another side to that work that didn’t show up in the metrics.

To build these automations, we had to sit with the people doing the jobs.

We would watch them work.
Ask questions.
Understand every step.

And then… replace it.

Sometimes, people were on our side.

They knew the work was repetitive.
They were frustrated by it.
They wanted to spend their time on something more meaningful.

Those conversations felt collaborative.
Almost optimistic.

Other times, it was very different.

TELUS was—and still is—a heavily unionized environment.

When I first started, I was part of that union.

But when I moved into a business analyst role, I crossed an invisible line.

  • Suddenly I was no longer part of “them.”
  • I was part of “management.”

Even though my day-to-day work didn’t feel that different, the perception did.

And that perception mattered.

There was a very real “us vs. them” dynamic.

For many of the people we worked with, the concern wasn’t abstract.

It was direct:

“Are you here to take my job?”

Those conversations were uncomfortable.

Painfully so.

You’re sitting across from someone, asking them to walk you through their work in detail… knowing that the outcome of that conversation might make their role unnecessary.

There wasn’t a clean way to reconcile that.

Over time, I started to feel like our team was being used to do something no one really wanted to own.

We weren’t just improving processes.

We were removing people from them.

Trusting the Wrong Signals

Our manager didn’t seem concerned.

He framed everything as progress.

Efficiency.
Optimization.
The future of the business.

And in a broad sense, he wasn’t wrong.

This was the direction things were moving.

But something about it didn’t sit well with me.

Not just the impact on people—but the way we were doing it.

  • no real engineering practices
  • no long-term thinking
  • no concern for maintainability
  • just results that could be reported upward

At the time, I didn’t fully understand why it felt off.

But I could feel it.

I didn’t trust that feeling.

Instead, I deferred to authority.

He was confident.
He gave compelling presentations.
He sounded like someone who knew what he was doing.

So I assumed he did.

Looking back, I see it differently.

He wasn’t a software engineer.
He wasn’t thinking about systems, maintainability, or long-term impact.

He was thinking about efficiency.

Cost reduction.

Outcomes he could present to the business.

And we were the mechanism that made that possible.

Doing Everything Means Carrying Everything

On that team, there were no clear roles.

We did everything:

  • development
  • testing
  • deployment
  • support

If something broke, it was on you.

There was no buffer.

That kind of ownership is powerful.

But it comes at a cost.

I worked long hours.

Nights.
Weekends.

I brought my laptop everywhere because I didn’t trust the systems to hold together without constant attention.

At some point, without really noticing it happening, I crossed into burnout.

The First Big Decision

Leaving didn’t feel like a clean, confident decision.

It didn’t feel like:

“I’ve learned what I need to learn, time to move on.”

It felt more like:

“I can’t keep doing this.”

I didn’t line up another job first.

I just left.

At the time, that felt reckless.

Looking back, it was overdue.

What I Took With Me

That period shaped me more than I realized at the time.

It taught me:

that automation can transform entire workflows
that speed without structure creates fragile systems
that real-world impact matters more than theoretical correctness
and that not trusting your instincts can keep you in the wrong place longer than you should stay

It also introduced something that would follow me for years:

Imposter syndrome.

Not in a dramatic way.

In a quiet, persistent way.

I started to develop a pattern.

Whenever something didn’t feel right, I didn’t trust that feeling.

Instead, I looked outward.

I assumed someone else knew better.

Someone more experienced.
More confident.
More certain.

I kept waiting to meet people who clearly understood what they were doing.

Real experts.

People who had it figured out.

At TELUS, I thought I had found one.

Someone who spoke confidently.
Presented well.
Seemed sure of the direction we were heading.

So I followed that signal.

Even when it didn’t fully sit right.

At the time, that felt like the responsible thing to do.

Trust the people who seem to know.

Looking back, I see it differently.

But I didn’t have that perspective yet.

What I Didn’t Learn (Yet)

What I didn’t do at that point was step back and ask:

What kind of work do I actually want to be doing?

Or more importantly:

What do I actually believe is right?

Instead, I made my next move the same way I made decisions up to that point:

By looking outward.

And that decision led me to my next chapter.
