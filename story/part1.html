Part 1: Stumbling Into Tech, Automating Myself Out of a Job, and Learning the Wrong Lessons First

I didn’t set out to build a career in software.

I didn’t have a plan, and I definitely didn’t have some grand sense of direction. Mostly, I needed a job. That job came through TELUS.

My dad worked there, and like a lot of people starting out, that connection helped me get my foot in the door. My interview lasted maybe twenty minutes. I don’t remember being particularly impressive. I just didn’t say anything that disqualified me, and that turned out to be enough.

At the time, I had no idea that I was stepping into the first chapter of a career that would end up shaping not just how I thought about software, but how I thought about work, authority, self-doubt, and the very long process of learning to trust my own instincts.

The Job That Didn’t Feel Like a Career

I started in Local Number Portability at a time when telecom competition was beginning to open up. On paper, the job sounded technical. In reality, it was mostly repetitive, manual work.

A typical task meant opening one system, copying information, pasting it into another, and then repeating that process across fifteen or twenty different tools. Over and over. All day.

Once you understood the process, it didn’t require much thinking. It was tedious, predictable, and honestly kind of numbing. But it paid well — much better than most jobs my friends had — and it allowed me to put myself through university without taking on debt. At that stage of my life, that mattered more than whether the work felt meaningful.

So I stayed.

The Moment Things Changed

Near the end of my computer science degree, something shifted.

It wasn’t dramatic. There wasn’t a cinematic moment where the whole world looked different. It was quieter than that. I simply started to notice the pattern behind the work.

Every order followed the same structure. The same kinds of inputs. The same kinds of outputs. The same sequence of steps.

And the tools we were using — mostly terminal-based systems — had just enough flexibility to support scripting.

So I started experimenting.

At first, it was small things: automating a few keystrokes, reducing repetitive clicks, shaving off tiny bits of friction. Nothing especially sophisticated. But once you start seeing repetitive work as something that can be reduced or eliminated, it becomes very hard to stop looking for more of it.

The small optimizations turned into bigger ones. Then entire workflows. Then eventually, almost everything I had been doing manually became automated.

At the time, I didn’t think of it as especially impressive. It just felt like the obvious thing to do. If something is repetitive, why would you keep doing it by hand if you can make the machine do it for you?

But the impact was impossible to ignore. My output went from normal to absurd. I was completing work at a rate that didn’t make sense compared to the rest of the team — something like one hundred times more than the top performers.

At first, my manager assumed the reporting system was broken. When I explained what I had done, I expected to get in trouble. I thought maybe I had violated some unwritten rule. Instead, she told me to keep going.

That moment mattered more than I realized at the time. It was the first time I seriously thought, maybe I actually know what I’m doing.

The Quick Wins Team

Not long after that, I was introduced to a small internal team that was working on the same kind of problem I had stumbled into on my own: automating inefficient processes across the company.

Their name said everything: Quick Wins.

Their approach was simple. Find something slow. Automate it. Move on.

There were no long planning cycles. No grand system redesigns. No deep architectural debates. Just obvious business inefficiencies and the pressure to remove them quickly.

I joined the team, and for the first time, the work felt genuinely exciting.

We worked directly with frontline employees, watching how they did their jobs and looking for opportunities to improve things. When we found something, we built a solution — fast.

We didn’t wait for perfect designs. We didn’t follow structured processes. We didn’t have real QA cycles. Sometimes testing happened directly in production.

Looking back, it was the definition of what I would later learn is called cowboy coding.

At the time, though, it just felt like momentum. The business loved us. We moved quickly. We delivered visible results. We were solving real problems in ways people could see almost immediately.

From the outside, it looked like success.

The Part That Didn’t Feel Right

There was another side to that work that didn’t show up in the metrics.

To build these automations, we had to sit with the people doing the jobs. We would watch them work, ask questions, and understand every step of what they did.

And then we would replace it.

Sometimes, people were completely on our side. They knew the work was repetitive. They were frustrated by it. They wanted to spend their time on something more complex and meaningful. Those conversations felt collaborative, even optimistic.

Other times, it was very different.

TELUS was — and still is — a heavily unionized environment. When I first started, I was part of that union. But once I moved into a business analyst role, I crossed an invisible line. Suddenly I was no longer part of “them.” I was part of “management.”

Even though my day-to-day work didn’t feel radically different, the perception did. And that perception mattered.

There was a very real us versus them dynamic in the company, and for many of the people we worked with, the concern wasn’t abstract. It was direct:

Are you here to take my job?

Those conversations were uncomfortable. Painfully so.

You are sitting across from someone, asking them to walk you through their work in detail, knowing that the outcome of that conversation might make their role unnecessary. There wasn’t a clean way to reconcile that.

Over time, I started to feel like our team was being used to do something no one really wanted to own. We weren’t just improving processes. We were removing people from them.

Trusting the Wrong Signals

Our manager didn’t seem particularly concerned by any of this. He framed everything as progress. Efficiency. Optimization. The future of the business.

And in a broad sense, he wasn’t wrong. This was where the world was moving. More automation. Fewer manual processes. Lower costs. Faster operations.

But something about it didn’t sit well with me.

Not just the impact on people, but the way we were doing it. There were no real engineering practices, no long-term thinking, no concern for maintainability, no meaningful sense that the software itself mattered beyond the results it could produce and the efficiency gains that could be reported upward.

At the time, I didn’t fully understand why it felt off. But I could feel it.

The problem was that I didn’t trust that feeling. Instead, I deferred to authority.

He was confident. He gave good presentations. He sounded like someone who knew exactly what he was doing. So I assumed he did.

Looking back, I see that differently. He wasn’t a software engineer. He wasn’t thinking about systems, maintainability, or long-term technical consequences. He was thinking about efficiency, cost reduction, and business outcomes he could present to other people.

And we were the mechanism that made that possible.

That mattered because it introduced a pattern that would stay with me for a very long time:

When I didn’t trust myself, I trusted the most confident voice in the room.

Doing Everything Means Carrying Everything

On that team, there were no clear roles.

We did everything: development, testing, deployment, support. If something broke, it was on you. There was no buffer.

That kind of ownership is powerful. It can teach you a lot. But it also comes at a cost.

I worked long hours. Nights. Weekends. I brought my laptop everywhere because I didn’t trust the systems to hold together without constant attention.

At some point, without really noticing it happening, I crossed into burnout.

That was not how I would have described it at the time. At the time, it just felt like exhaustion, irritability, and the growing certainty that I could not keep doing things the way we were doing them. But looking back, that’s exactly what it was.

The First Big Decision

Leaving didn’t feel like a clean, confident decision.

It didn’t feel like I had completed some chapter and was moving on wisely. It felt more like I had reached the point where I simply couldn’t keep going.

I didn’t line up another job first. I just left.

At the time, that felt reckless. Looking back, it was overdue.

That period shaped me more than I realized at the time. It taught me that automation can transform entire workflows. It taught me that speed without structure creates fragile systems. It taught me that real-world impact matters more than theoretical correctness. And it taught me — though I wouldn’t fully understand it until much later — that not trusting your instincts can keep you in the wrong place much longer than you should stay.

It also introduced something else that would follow me for years: imposter syndrome.

Not in a dramatic, obvious way. In a quiet, persistent way.

Whenever something didn’t feel right, I didn’t trust that feeling. I looked outward instead. I assumed someone else knew better. Someone more experienced. More confident. More certain.

I kept waiting to meet people who clearly understood what they were doing. Real experts. People who had it figured out.

At TELUS, I thought I had found one. Someone who spoke confidently, presented well, and seemed sure of the direction we were heading. So I followed that signal, even when it didn’t fully sit right.

At the time, that felt like the responsible thing to do. Trust the people who seem to know.

Looking back, I see it differently. But I didn’t have that perspective yet.

What I Didn’t Learn (Yet)

What I didn’t do at that point was step back and ask the deeper questions.

What kind of work do I actually want to be doing?

What do I actually believe is right?

Instead, I made my next move the same way I had made decisions up to that point: by looking outward.

And that decision led me to the next chapter.
