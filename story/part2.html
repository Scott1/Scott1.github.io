Part 2: Learning “Real” Engineering… and Realizing It Still Wasn’t Quite Right

After leaving TELUS, I told myself a simple story. The problem wasn’t me. The problem was the environment.

It was too chaotic. Too reactive. Too improvised. We were building software quickly, but not well. We were getting results, but I didn’t trust the foundation. So when I left, I believed I was leaving behind the wrong environment.

The answer, I thought, was to find the opposite. I wanted somewhere bigger. More established. More disciplined. Somewhere with experts. Somewhere I could finally learn how software was actually supposed to be built.

That search led me to Symantec.

Getting In — and Still Feeling Like I Didn’t Belong

Even applying to Symantec felt intimidating. My imposter syndrome was already well established by then, even if I didn’t have a name for it yet.

The interviews were serious, but not impossible. I got a lot of questions right. The ones I didn’t know, I admitted I didn’t know. Somehow, that didn’t disqualify me. I got the job.

A More Professional World

Compared to TELUS, Symantec immediately felt more “real.” There were developers, leads, managers, directors, architects, QA teams, release engineering teams, technical support teams — an actual software organization.

I joined a team working on Symantec Protection Network, or SPN. I was junior compared to most of the people around me, so I took on whatever work was available. That work turned out to be the installer.

Owning the Least Glamorous Part

The installer was not the exciting part of the product. It wasn’t malware detection. It wasn’t cloud infrastructure. It was the first thing a user encountered when installing the software on their machine.

I worked with C++, InstallShield, and MSBuild. I wrote code around installer logic and custom actions. I also touched some client-side code and local storage using SQLite, but the installer remained my main responsibility.

The Problem With “Proper Engineering”

For a while, I told myself I had found what I was looking for. The environment was more structured. The product was more serious. The company was clearly capable of building and shipping software at scale.

But after a while, something familiar started to creep in. That same uneasy feeling. Something still didn’t feel quite right.

The teams were siloed. I had expected mentorship and a clear path to growth. Instead, I was given work and expected to complete it. I was still, in many ways, learning alone — just inside a more polished building.

The Architecture Clue

My team lead once took me and another developer out after work to explain the product and the broader system. I tried to follow, but at some point, it all blurred together. So I asked him to draw it.

Once he started sketching it out — boxes, lines, arrows — it started making sense. Not just to me. The conversation got sharper for everyone.

Looking back, that moment mattered. It was one of the first times I saw that I naturally wanted a higher-level map of the system.

What I Took With Me

My time at Symantec taught me that structure doesn’t guarantee clarity. Process doesn’t guarantee quality. Large organizations can be professional and still deeply fragmented.

I was still looking for experts. Still assuming someone else had the full picture. That pattern would follow me into the next chapter. Only this time, I really would find experts. And I would still feel just as lost.
