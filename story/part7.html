Part 7: Stepping Away, Letting the Noise Settle, and Trying to Figure Out What Actually Matters

When I left Cisco, it did not feel triumphant.

It didn’t feel like I had cracked some code, or finally figured everything out, or made some bold and confident move toward the future I had always wanted.

It felt uncertain.

In some ways, it even felt embarrassing.

For most of my adult life, I had always had the next thing lined up in some form. One role led to the next. One company gave way to another. Even when I was unhappy, even when I was restless, even when I knew something was off, there was still a structure holding me. A title. A team. A paycheck. A direction, even if it wasn’t really my direction.

Leaving removed all of that at once.

And when the motion stopped, when there was finally space, a question showed up that I had managed to avoid for a very long time:

Who am I when I’m not inside the machine?

The First Real Pause

For the first time since graduating from the University of Calgary, I didn’t have a clear next step.

No job lined up.
No immediate role to move into.
No externally validated story to tell about what came next.

Just space.

At first, that space felt uncomfortable.

Actually, more than uncomfortable — it felt unnatural.

I had spent more than twenty years living inside systems that always gave me something to react to. A backlog. A deadline. A release. A feature request. A team dynamic. A manager. An expectation. Even when I didn’t like the direction I was going, the path itself was still visible because the system around me kept generating it.

Without that, the silence was loud.

At first there was relief, of course. Relief from deadlines, from the organizational chaos, from the bureaucratic friction, from the constant low-level stress of being in environments that no longer felt aligned. But once that relief settled, something else emerged.

Stillness.

And in that stillness, there was much less to hide behind.

Looking Back More Honestly Than I Ever Had Before

When you are in a job — especially in software, especially in a demanding environment — it is very easy to keep your attention fixed on the next thing. The next feature. The next technical problem. The next deadline. The next reorg. The next role. Even your dissatisfaction can become part of the motion. It gives you something to push against.

But once I stepped away, I had a different kind of opportunity.

For the first time, I could really look back.

Not in a polished “career reflection” kind of way. Not in a “what are my strengths and weaknesses” kind of way. Not as some exercise meant to generate talking points for an interview.

I mean honestly.

Looking at my life and my career and asking:

Why did I keep making the choices I made?
What patterns kept repeating?
What had actually energized me?
What had drained me?
What did I keep tolerating?
What was I always moving toward?
What was I always avoiding?

Those were not comfortable questions.

But they were finally available.

And the more I sat with them, the more a pattern started to emerge.

How Often I Followed Signals Instead of Myself

One of the hardest things to admit was how often I had made decisions based on external signals rather than internal clarity.

For most of my career, when I didn’t know what to do, I looked outward.

I looked for:

  • the confident voice
  • the persuasive manager
  • the company with the strongest reputation
  • the team that seemed smartest
  • the role that looked like the “right next move”
  • the opportunity other people would approve of

And then I followed that.

Sometimes it led me somewhere useful. Sometimes it led me somewhere harmful. But the deeper problem was that I wasn’t really asking myself what I wanted, what I believed, or what felt aligned. I was looking for evidence outside myself that would tell me what the correct move was.

That pattern had been there all along.

At TELUS, I followed confidence and authority over my own discomfort.
At Symantec, I followed the promise of “real engineering.”
At Immunet, I followed the pull of real experts and meaningful work, even though I was terrified.
At Cisco, I kept trying to make each new phase work by assuming the next structural change or technical shift would solve the deeper mismatch.

Again and again, I outsourced the question of direction.

That worked well enough to keep my career moving.

It did not work well enough to help me build a life that felt truly aligned.

Fear Was Underneath More Than I Wanted to Admit

The more honestly I looked at this pattern, the more obvious it became that fear was underneath it.

Not just general anxiety, though there was plenty of that.

A more specific fear:

fear of being wrong
fear of making the wrong move
fear of failing visibly
fear of exposing myself as less capable than others
fear of choosing something unconventional and finding out I wasn’t good enough to make it work

That fear shaped more of my career than I had wanted to admit.

It was part of why I stayed in roles longer than I should have. Part of why I resisted leadership opportunities. Part of why I overvalued certainty. Part of why I kept trying to learn everything before acting. Part of why I kept assuming other people knew better than I did.

Fear made caution feel like wisdom.

Sometimes it was.

Often it was just fear.

And the problem with fear when it becomes your compass is that it doesn’t point toward what matters. It points toward what feels safest.

That realization was hard.

Because I could see that many of the decisions I had told myself were rational, practical, mature decisions had also been shaped by avoidance.

I had often chosen safety over alignment.

And then I had worked very hard to make that safety feel meaningful.

Meditation Changed Meaning Once There Was More Space

By this point, meditation was no longer a side interest.

It was a real part of my life.

During my working years, especially in the Immunet, Sourcefire, and Cisco periods, meditation had become important. It had given me glimpses. It had softened my relationship to imposter syndrome. It had helped me see that my thoughts and reactions were not the whole of who I was. It had given me moments of calm, connection, and a broader sense of being that stood in contrast to the very anxious, ego-driven mental world I had lived in for so long.

But once I stepped away from full-time work, the practice changed again.

There was more room for it.

More time.
Less noise.
Less constant reactivity.

And with that, some of the lessons of meditation stopped being purely occasional insights and started becoming more lived, more integrated.

I could see more clearly how quickly the mind creates a story. How quickly it says:

  • this is who I am
  • this is what this means
  • this is dangerous
  • this is failure
  • this is success
  • this is the right path

And because I had more space, I could also see how often those stories were not true — or at the very least, not the only way to understand what was happening.

The idea of the ego, which had once felt abstract and philosophical, became deeply personal.

I could see:

  • the part of me that wanted to look competent
  • the part that wanted others to approve of me
  • the part that compared constantly
  • the part that feared being diminished
  • the part that built an identity out of success, capability, and not being exposed

And I could also see that those patterns were not fixed.

They were strong.
They were habitual.
They were persuasive.

But they were not absolute.

That changed something fundamental.

The Gap Got Bigger

Earlier in my story, I talked about the gap that started appearing between a trigger and my response.

That gap kept growing.

Not perfectly. Not permanently. Not in some magical “I am now free of ego” kind of way.

But enough to matter.

Enough that I could increasingly see my inner world happening in real time instead of being completely fused with it.

A thought would arise.
A fear would arise.
A story about myself would start forming.

And now, more often than before, I could see it as a pattern instead of as reality.

That doesn’t sound dramatic.

But it is.

Because once you can see a pattern, you are no longer completely trapped inside it.

That’s where real change starts.

Not in forcing yourself to become someone else.
Not in punishing yourself for still having fear.
Not in pretending you’re beyond all of it.

But in seeing clearly enough that you have some freedom in how you respond.

That became one of the most important things I have ever learned.

Looking at My Career as a Pattern, Not Just a Timeline

Once I started seeing my internal patterns more clearly, I also started seeing my career differently.

For most of my life, I had thought about my career in the usual way: a timeline of jobs, companies, technologies, promotions, opportunities, and disappointments.

Now I started seeing it as something else.

A pattern of behavior.

And in that pattern, I could see things I had never quite named before.

I could see where I stayed small.

I could see where I avoided opportunities because they carried too much uncertainty.

I could see where I did not trust my own instincts.

I could see where I forced myself into roles or modes of work that didn’t align with my deeper strengths, simply because they seemed like the “correct” thing to do.

But I could also see another pattern.

A more hopeful one.

When had I felt most alive?
When had time disappeared?
When had I felt useful in a way that went deeper than just finishing a task?

Again and again, the answer was not exactly what my titles would suggest.

It showed up in:

  • helping people align
  • making a system understandable
  • working through ambiguity with others
  • turning a messy problem into something clear
  • feeling close to a product that actually mattered
  • sensing meaning in the work rather than just output

That was revealing.

Because it suggested that the most important parts of what I had contributed over the years were not always the parts I had formally been hired to do.

AI Started to Feel Like a Pull, Not Just an Interest

Around this same time, I found myself increasingly drawn toward artificial intelligence.

At first, it was curiosity.

I was reading. Watching. Paying attention. Trying to understand what was happening, what was real, what was hype, and what was actually changing.

But the more I paid attention, the more something in me woke up.

This didn’t feel like just another technical trend.

It felt meaningful.

Not only because it was technically impressive, but because it seemed to open the door to the kind of work I had been wanting for a long time without fully knowing how to name it.

AI had the potential to reshape:

how people learn
how information becomes accessible
how decisions are supported
how mental and physical health can be understood and improved
how systems that were previously too complex to navigate might become more human and useful

For the first time in a long time, I didn’t feel pushed by fear or pulled by external expectation.

I felt pulled by genuine interest.

That was a different feeling.

A healthier feeling.

And I trusted it more than I had trusted many things in my career.

Not Rushing Into the Next Label

One of the hardest things about this period was resisting the urge to immediately turn all of these reflections into a new, neatly packaged identity.

That impulse was strong.

Okay, I’ve left.
Okay, I’ve reflected.
Okay, I’m interested in AI.
So what am I now? What’s the title? What’s the lane? What’s the plan?

That urge makes sense. It’s how careers are supposed to work. You leave one thing and define the next.

But I didn’t want to do that too quickly.

Because one of the things I had been learning through meditation, reflection, and stepping away was that my old pattern had been to rush toward the next externally coherent identity before I had really listened to myself.

So this time, I tried not to.

I allowed myself to not know.

That was uncomfortable.

It still is, sometimes.

But it also felt more honest than pretending I had already mapped out the future.

What Matters to Me Now

Looking back across everything — TELUS, Symantec, Immunet, Sourcefire, Cisco, and the time after — I can see certain things much more clearly now than I ever could while I was in the middle of them.

I am not only interested in building software.

I am interested in:

  • solving meaningful problems
  • helping make complex systems understandable
  • working with people
  • reducing confusion
  • contributing to something that actually matters in the world

I still care about technology. Deeply.

I still enjoy learning technical things. I still like understanding how systems work. I still like solving hard problems.

But I no longer believe the only meaningful way I can contribute is by sitting alone in front of a machine writing code for hours.

That matters.

Because for much of my life, I thought that was the only kind of contribution that counted.

Now I can see that my value may lie more broadly:

  • around the technology
  • across the technology
  • between people and the technology
  • in the way systems and humans understand each other

And I’m finally willing to take that possibility seriously.

Where This Leaves Me

I am not at the end of the story.

I don’t have some perfectly resolved conclusion.

I’m not writing this from the perspective of someone who has fully transcended fear, ego, uncertainty, or confusion.

What I have is something more honest.

I have more awareness.
More willingness to trust my instincts.
More ability to sit in uncertainty without immediately trying to outsource the answer.
More respect for what has actually energized me, even when it didn’t fit the expected narrative.

And I have a stronger sense than ever that work matters most when it is aligned with something deeper than safety, prestige, or inertia.

That does not necessarily mean one specific role.

It might show up as:

  • engineering
  • leadership
  • product work
  • research
  • teaching
  • building
  • some hybrid path that I still can’t fully describe yet

But it has to feel real.

It has to feel connected to something meaningful.

And for the first time in my career, I feel more interested in following that than in following the strongest signal in the room.

What Comes Next

If there is a final lesson in all of this, it may be this:

There are experts. Real ones.

There are also people who perform expertise.

There are leaders who create clarity, and leaders who create the appearance of clarity.

There are systems that help people do meaningful work, and systems that slowly drain the meaning out of the work.

And there is a difference between learning what the world seems to reward and learning what actually matters to you.

It took me a very long time to start seeing that difference clearly.

Longer than I wish it had.

But I see it now.

And now that I do, I can’t really go back to pretending I don’t.
