Part 4: Scaling Up Without Losing Ourselves, and the First Time I Questioned Whether Coding Was Really My Thing

When Immunet was acquired by Sourcefire, it felt like a breakthrough. It felt like proof that what we had built mattered.

A bigger, respected security company had looked at what we had made and said: yes, this is real, this is valuable, and we want it.

The Enterprise Version Was Only the Beginning

By the time the acquisition closed, we had already spent an enormous amount of energy building the enterprise version of the product.

The enterprise version being “done” didn’t mean the work slowed down. It meant the next phase began immediately. More features, more polish, more control, more visibility, more robustness.

The Client Side Was Still My World

My work was still heavily centered on the client: installer, GUI, build process, client-side plumbing, endpoint behavior, and how information was surfaced to users and sent back into the broader system.

But the meaning of client-side work had changed. The endpoint was part of a larger system that depended on cloud intelligence, reporting pipelines, event visibility, policy enforcement, and coordination between multiple moving parts.

The Team Grew, and So Did My Unofficial Responsibilities

As the product became more successful, we grew. New office. More hiring. More people. And with that growth came a new kind of work for me.

I got more involved in interviewing, onboarding, explaining how pieces of the product worked, and helping bridge conversations between different parts of the organization.

At the time, I did not think of this as leadership. If anything, I saw it as dangerous. These were the moments where I felt like I might be exposed.

But once I was actually in those situations, I felt engaged. Helping someone understand something or helping a group get aligned energized me in a way sitting alone with a block of code often did not.

A Growing Sense That I Was Different

As we hired more people, I started noticing something else. A lot of them genuinely seemed to want to sit down and code. For me, that was becoming less and less true.

I could still do the work. But I increasingly noticed that I had to make myself sit down and do the deep implementation part in a way that others didn’t seem to.

The parts I naturally gravitated toward were helping people understand things, talking through system behavior, connecting ideas across teams, and clarifying what we were actually trying to build.

The Startup Culture Was Still Alive

The Sourcefire acquisition did not immediately kill what made Immunet special. For a while, it felt like the best of both worlds: more resources, more sales support, and a larger platform, while still feeling close to the product and each other.

That feeling created energy and meaning. Later, when that feeling started to disappear, I would understand how much my motivation had depended on it.

What This Period Taught Me

This phase pushed me deeper into security product development, richer endpoint visibility, event reporting, client-side architecture, performance-conscious engineering, and security-aware development.

It also sharpened a question: just because I can do this work, does that mean this is the work I’m actually best suited for?
