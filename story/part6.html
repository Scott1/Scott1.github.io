Part 6: Agile Without Owners, Carrying the Gaps, Burning Out, and Finally Walking Away

By the later years of my time at Cisco, the changes that had once felt subtle were no longer subtle at all.

What had started as a small, highly aligned startup team had gradually turned into something much larger, much more layered, and much harder to feel connected to.

From a Team to a Business Unit

Our original product team stopped feeling like a team and started functioning like a business unit. New teams were folded in. Managers became directors and VPs. Distance grew between decision-makers and implementers.

The Agile Transition

We formally transitioned into Agile methodologies — Scrum and Kanban. On paper, it made sense. The problem was not Agile itself. The problem was how we tried to implement it.

We did not bring in experienced Scrum Masters or strong Product Owners. Instead, we rotated developers and QA engineers through those roles.

The Scrum Master role required facilitation, alignment, conflict management, and momentum. The Product Owner role required clarity, product judgment, prioritization, and ownership.

Many of the brilliant technical people around me struggled with those things. That realization landed hard. Expertise was real, but it was also narrow.

The Large Cross-Team Project

The point where all of this crystallized was during a large cross-team project. It touched multiple parts of the system and required real coordination across groups.

Right around that time, our senior developer and team manager left. The project didn’t stop because the anchors had left. It kept moving.

I stepped into the biggest unofficial leadership role of my career: coordinating across teams, aligning people around what we were trying to build, untangling misunderstandings, facilitating decisions, and keeping the work moving.

The Real Burnout

This was where my real burnout happened: the senior developer leaving, broken Agile, me filling gaps without formal support, COVID hitting, and the old team disappearing.

I carried the project, the gaps, the confusion, the old context, and the new expectations for months. Eventually, I broke — not dramatically, but like a system overloaded too long.

The Move to Server-Side Work

Burnout led me to leave endpoint work, not Cisco. I moved into server-side development, which meant backend services, APIs, cloud systems, asynchronous processing, distributed systems, logging, monitoring, observability, and data movement across services.

I also had to think in terms of scale in a way I never had before: millions or billions of requests, traffic spikes, failed dependencies, degraded services, reliability, recovery, and performance across distributed systems.

I learned Go, AWS, Lambda, DynamoDB, SQS, API Gateway, S3, CloudWatch, X-Ray, Docker, JSON/YAML, backend service patterns, cloud-native design, and observability.

There was no formal training program. Senior developers pointed me at tutorials and examples, and then it was on me. Someone points toward the mountain, and I climb it myself.

Why I Finally Left

For about a year and a half, the newness carried me. But eventually the same underlying problem came back: lack of direction, too much bureaucracy, layers between the work and meaning, and a feeling that the machine mattered more than the product.

At the same time, AI was accelerating in a way that felt impossible to ignore. I had a strong gut feeling this was huge, and I didn’t want to watch something transformative pass me by while I played it safe.

So I left.

What This Chapter Taught Me

Process without ownership doesn’t work. Technical expertise does not automatically translate into leadership. Culture matters more than organizations realize. Burnout is not just too much work — it is too much weight without enough support or meaning.
