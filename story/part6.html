Part 6: Agile Without Owners, Carrying the Gaps, Burning Out, and Finally Walking Away

By the later years of my time at Cisco, the changes that had once felt subtle were no longer subtle at all.

What had started as a small, highly aligned, deeply capable startup team had gradually turned into something much larger, much more layered, and much harder to feel connected to. This didn’t happen all at once. That’s part of what made it difficult to understand while it was happening. There was never one clean break where I could point and say, this is the moment everything changed. Instead, it was a slow drift. A series of organizational shifts, role changes, new processes, new reporting structures, and new people that, over time, transformed the character of the work.

At first, I could still tell myself the essential parts were intact. The product still mattered. There were still strong people around. There was still momentum. But eventually the reality became impossible to ignore.

The startup culture that had carried so much of the meaning and energy of the work was fading.

And as it faded, the gaps that had always existed—but had once been held together by talent, urgency, and shared ownership—started opening wider and wider.

From a Team to a Business Unit

One of the most important shifts was structural.

Over time, our original product team stopped feeling like a team and started functioning like a business unit inside a much larger organization. New teams from within Cisco joined our orbit. Teams from other acquisitions were folded in. The managers who had once been close to the work were promoted into broader leadership positions, with more people reporting to them and more products under their scope. They became directors and VPs. Their responsibilities grew, which meant their distance from the day-to-day reality of our specific product grew too.

That wasn’t necessarily anyone’s fault. It was just the natural effect of scale.

But it changed the shape of things.

The people who really understood the product deeply—how it had been built, why certain decisions had been made, where the hidden traps were, what mattered most to customers, what the culture of the team had once been—were now a smaller and smaller percentage of the total group involved in moving the work forward.

And with that came distance.

Distance between decision-makers and implementers. Distance between customers and developers. Distance between the people who had built something and the people now responsible for managing and evolving it.

That distance mattered.

Because once it appears, other things fill the space.

Usually process. Usually hierarchy. Usually interpretation.

The Agile Transition

It was during this period that we formally transitioned into Agile methodologies—Scrum and Kanban.

On paper, it made sense.

The team was larger now. The work was more distributed. There were more dependencies, more moving pieces, more need for coordination, more need for visibility into what was being worked on and when. We were no longer a small enough group to simply rely on shared context and informal communication.

So we transitioned.

At least, we tried to.

The problem was not Agile itself. The problem was how we tried to implement it.

We did not hire anyone with real experience in the most critical roles. We did not bring in seasoned Scrum Masters. We did not bring in strong Product Owners who deeply understood the product, the customers, the decision-making responsibilities, and the importance of quick, clear prioritization.

Instead, we tried to create those roles by rotating people from development and QA through them.

At first, that seemed practical. Who better to understand the work than the people already doing it?

But in reality, it exposed something I had not fully understood before.

The Scrum Master role required a very particular set of skills:

  • facilitating alignment across different personalities
  • keeping conversations productive
  • making sure priorities stayed visible
  • helping teams navigate ambiguity and conflict
  • pushing things forward even when no one was entirely comfortable

The Product Owner role required something else entirely:

  • clarity about what mattered most
  • the ability to make fast trade-offs
  • enough confidence to say “this is what we’re doing next”
  • enough product understanding to make those calls well
  • enough ownership to stand behind the direction

And what became obvious, quickly, was that many of the brilliant technical people around me—people I had admired for years, people I had compared myself to and felt inferior to—were not naturally good at those things.

They struggled.

They struggled with leadership.
They struggled with product judgment.
They struggled with people alignment.
They struggled with ambiguity of a social and organizational kind.

Some of them were phenomenal engineers. Deeply capable. Much stronger than me in certain technical areas.

But this was different work.

And that realization landed hard.

Because for so much of my career, I had made a quiet assumption that technical brilliance implied a more universal kind of capability. That if someone was better than me in one area, they were probably better than me in all the others too. Better at leadership. Better at communication. Better at making decisions. Better at knowing what should happen.

Now I could see that wasn’t true.

Expertise was real.

But it was also narrow.

Outside of their domain, people were just people again.

Trying things. Fumbling. Filling space. Guessing more than they wanted to admit.

That was important.

Because it changed how I saw the so-called experts around me.

And it changed, slowly, how I saw myself.

Stepping Into the Gaps

As these new Agile structures struggled to take hold, the gaps became impossible to miss.

There were gaps in ownership.

Gaps in clarity.

Gaps in prioritization.

Gaps in actual leadership, even when people held leadership-sounding roles.

And because those gaps weren’t being effectively filled by the formal process, they had to be filled informally.

That is where I found myself once again.

Not by title.

Not because I had been promoted into some official authority.

But because the work required it.

I found myself stepping into conversations that no one else seemed to be steering. I found myself helping teams understand each other, translating between people who were using the same words to mean different things, helping surface what the real problem actually was, or what the actual decision needed to be.

I found myself doing the work of alignment.

Again.

And the bigger the organization got, the more valuable and the more exhausting that became.

Because now the stakes were higher, the people were more distributed, the context was less shared, and the misunderstandings traveled farther before anyone caught them.

The Large Cross-Team Project

The point where all of this really crystallized was during a large cross-team project.

This was not a small feature. It wasn’t the kind of thing one person or one sub-team could quietly implement without anyone else needing to care. It touched multiple parts of the system, involved multiple teams, and required real coordination across different groups with different understandings, different priorities, and different assumptions.

And right around that time, our senior developer and team manager left.

That timing mattered.

Because those were exactly the kinds of people who normally held these complicated things together. They carried historical context. They had authority. They knew where to make decisions, how to interpret trade-offs, and how to guide a large body of work through ambiguity.

Then suddenly, they were gone.

And the project didn’t stop just because the anchors had left.

It kept moving.

Which meant the gaps had to be filled by someone.

That was where I stepped into the biggest unofficial leadership role of my career.

I started helping coordinate across teams, aligning people around what we were actually trying to build, helping untangle misunderstandings, facilitating decision-making, and keeping the thing moving.

This wasn’t just “being helpful.”

This was real responsibility, even if no one formalized it that way.

I was not just contributing. I was helping hold together a piece of work that otherwise could have drifted or fractured.

And the strange thing was, internally, I still did not feel ready.

I still had all the same internal patterns:

  • uncertainty
  • self-doubt
  • fear of being exposed
  • fear that I was somehow not the right person for this

But the work didn’t care how I felt.

It needed someone to help move it.

So I moved it.

And that taught me something important about leadership.

Leadership wasn’t what I had imagined earlier in my career.

It wasn’t having all the answers.
It wasn’t being the smartest person in the room.
It wasn’t projecting certainty.

It was helping people align, creating clarity where there wasn’t any, and making forward movement possible even when the information was incomplete.

I had been doing pieces of that work for years.

This was the first time it became unavoidable.

The Real Burnout

This was where my real burnout happened.

Not later. Not after I left the client side. Not as some vague result of “too much work over too many years.”

It happened here, in the middle of that combination of pressures:

  • our senior developer leaving and the resulting leadership vacuum
  • the broken Agile transition and all the gaps it created
  • me stepping into those gaps over and over without formal support
  • COVID hitting
  • and then, as if that weren’t enough, the old team really starting to disappear

That last part mattered enormously.

COVID changed the way everyone worked, of course. But it also coincided with the collapse of the legacy team structure that had held so much context and culture. One by one, and then more quickly, the people from the original Immunet days and the Sourcefire growth years left the company.

The people I had leaned on.

The people who knew the history.

The people who carried the deeper context.

The people who, even if they weren’t perfect leaders or perfect mentors, were part of the cultural and technical memory of the system.

They were gone.

And what that left behind was not just more work.

It left isolation.

I was carrying more and more context, more and more unofficial responsibility, more and more alignment work, and fewer and fewer people remained who I could lean on, check with, or share the burden with.

So I pushed.

For months, I pushed.

I carried the project. I carried the gaps. I carried the confusion. I carried the old context and the new expectations and the growing machine that no longer felt like a team.

And eventually, I broke.

Not dramatically. Not with one explosive moment.

More like a system that has been overloaded too long and finally starts failing in all the predictable ways.

That was my burnout.

The Move to Server-Side Work

Burnout is what led me to leave the endpoint work.

Not the company.

That’s an important distinction.

I was still at Cisco. I just knew I could not keep carrying what I had been carrying on the client side. I needed a change. A new environment. New problems. New technologies. Some place where I could put down some of the weight I had accumulated and start again in a different technical space.

So I moved into server-side development.

Technically, it was a major shift.

For most of my career up to that point, I had been rooted in endpoint and client-side thinking:

  • software running on a single machine
  • system-level behavior
  • installers
  • GUI logic
  • performance on the endpoint
  • interactions between local components and a broader system

Now I had to think differently.

The server-side world pulled me into:

backend services
APIs
cloud systems
asynchronous processing
distributed systems
logging, monitoring, and observability
data movement across services
debugging problems that didn’t exist on one machine, but across multiple systems talking to each other

And just as importantly, I had to start thinking in terms of scale in a way I never had before.

Endpoint work naturally keeps your attention close to the machine: what is happening here, on this system, under these conditions, with this process, this driver, this installer, this update flow.

Server-side work stretches that perspective outward.

Now I had to think about questions like:

what happens when this service gets millions of requests?
what happens when it gets billions?
what happens when traffic spikes unexpectedly?
what happens when one dependency slows down or fails?
how do these systems degrade under load?
how do you reason about performance, reliability, and recovery when the work is spread across multiple services and environments?

That was a different mental model entirely.

It wasn’t just:

“What is this program doing?”

It became:

“What is this system doing at scale, under pressure, across many moving parts?”

I also had to learn a whole set of technologies and tools that were either completely new to me or much more central than they had ever been before.

That included things like:

  • Go / Golang
  • AWS
  • Lambda
  • DynamoDB
  • SQS
  • API Gateway
  • S3
  • CloudWatch
  • X-Ray
  • Docker
  • JSON / YAML
  • backend service patterns
  • cloud-native design
  • distributed architecture concerns
  • observability and tracing

And just like earlier chapters of my career, there was no formal training program waiting for me.

No structured onboarding curriculum.
No courses the company sent me through.
No carefully designed development path.

What I got, once again, was some combination of:

  • senior developers pointing me at tutorials
  • documentation
  • examples
  • rough guidance on where to start

And then it was on me.

I had to learn it the way I had learned so many other things in my career: by reading, experimenting, struggling, asking what questions I could, and figuring out as much as possible on my own while still being expected to contribute.

That pattern was familiar by then.

Someone points toward the mountain.

And I climb it myself.

For a while, that was exciting.

It really was.

It was another growth phase, technically. Another period where I was stretched in useful ways. It brought back some of the energy that comes from learning something new and feeling your brain expand around it.

For about a year and a half, that newness carried me.

Why I Finally Left

But eventually, the same underlying problem came back.

The chaos I had felt on the client side wasn’t limited to the client side.

It started appearing in the server work too.

Not in exactly the same form, but in the same deeper pattern:

  • lack of direction
  • too much bureaucracy
  • layers between the work and the meaning of the work
  • a feeling that the machine mattered more than the product
  • and a growing sense that this was simply how Cisco operated

That was hard to accept.

Because for a while, I had hoped I just needed a technical reset. A new domain. A fresh challenge.

But once the excitement of new server-side work wore off, I felt it again.

That same feeling of being a cog in a machine.

Turning out the next thing.
Shipping the next widget.
Serving the needs of the organization more than the needs of something that felt meaningful.

And at the same time, something else was happening in the world.

AI was accelerating.

Not quietly. Not as some distant curiosity. It was taking off in a way that felt profound and obvious to me. I could feel, viscerally, that this technology was going to matter. Not just in the sense that it would create new products, but in the sense that it was going to transform how we work, how we build, how we think, maybe even how we live.

And while I kept trying to bring that energy and urgency into the conversations around me at Cisco, I felt like almost no one around me was truly taking it seriously. Or if they were, it was in a very passive, procedural, corporate kind of way.

I had a strong gut feeling that this was huge.

And I couldn’t shake it.

I felt like I was being left behind.

That if I stayed where I was—playing it safe, pushing out widgets for the Cisco machine, accepting the bureaucracy as normal—I would be watching something transformative pass me by while I kept telling myself that stability was the responsible choice.

And I didn’t want that.

At some point, the pull became stronger than my fear.

And I left.

What This Chapter Taught Me

This phase of my career brought together almost everything I had been learning for years.

It taught me that process without ownership doesn’t work.
That technical expertise does not automatically translate into leadership.
That culture matters more than most organizations realize, and once it starts to collapse, no amount of process can fully replace it.
That unofficial leadership can become very real responsibility, even when no one formally acknowledges it.
That burnout is not just about too much work, but about too much weight carried without enough support or meaning.

It also sharpened my understanding of experts.

By this point, the story had become more complicated.

I had met real experts. They existed. I had seen what true technical capability looked like. I had seen how much aligned experts could accomplish together.

But I had also now seen another category emerge very clearly: the organizational experts. The people who looked authoritative because they were good in meetings, or good at navigating hierarchy, or good at sounding certain, even when the certainty was not well grounded. People who could carry titles and influence and still not really know how to move the actual work forward.

And that taught me something important.

There are real experts.
There are also people performing expertise.
And in large organizations, it is not always easy to tell the difference.

By the end of this chapter, I had also finally done something I had failed to do many times before:

I listened to myself.

Not perfectly. Not early enough. But eventually, I did.

And that changed what came next.
