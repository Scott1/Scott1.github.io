A Short Version of My Career Story

I didn’t set out to build a career in software.

I got my start at TELUS doing repetitive, manual work, copying data between systems all day. It wasn’t glamorous, but it paid well and helped me get through university.

At some point, I noticed the pattern. The work was repetitive. Predictable. Automatable. So I started writing scripts to do it for me.

What began as small optimizations turned into full automation. My output increased dramatically, and for the first time, I thought:

Maybe I actually know what I’m doing.

That led me to an internal team focused on automation, fast-moving, high-impact work where we built solutions quickly and delivered real results.

But there was a downside. To automate the work, we had to sit with the people doing it and then replace it. At the same time, we were building software with almost no structure, no real processes, no long-term thinking. It worked, but something about it didn’t feel right.

I ignored that feeling. Instead, I trusted the most confident voice in the room. That became a pattern.

Looking for something more structured, I moved to Symantec.

There, I found “real” software engineering, teams, processes, and more disciplined development. I worked on installers, client-side systems, and learned what it meant to own part of a product.

But something still felt off. The work was fragmented. I owned pieces of a system I didn’t fully understand. There wasn’t much mentorship, and I found myself learning on my own again.

Still, I believed that somewhere out there were real experts, people who truly understood everything.

At Immunet, I finally found them.

This was a small, high-performing team building advanced malware protection software. Everyone seemed deeply knowledgeable. Discussions were sharp, fast, and grounded in real expertise.

And I was completely out of my depth.

Instead of asking questions, I stayed quiet. I learned by listening, observing, and piecing things together on my own. It worked, but it made everything harder than it needed to be.

At the same time, I was exposed to something new: startup culture at its best. We were close to the product, close to users, and everything we built felt meaningful.

As the company grew, first through acquisition by Sourcefire, then Cisco, the environment changed. Teams expanded. Structure increased. Distance from the product grew.

I also started noticing something about myself. While I could code, I didn’t love sitting down to write code the way others did. But I did love helping people understand systems, explaining ideas, aligning teams, and making complexity clear.

I began to see that my strengths might not be in writing code, but in working around it.

During this time, I also discovered something that would become foundational for me: mindfulness meditation.

At first, it felt like nothing. But over time, it gave me something I hadn’t experienced before, a small gap between what happened and how I reacted.

That gap changed how I saw everything. I began to question assumptions I had always accepted, including the idea that other people knew better than I did.

At Cisco, the startup culture eventually faded. We transitioned to Agile without strong leadership in key roles. Teams became more fragmented. The connection to meaningful work weakened.

At one point, I found myself in an unofficial leadership role during a large cross-team project, filling gaps left by departing leaders and trying to keep things aligned without real support.

That was when I burned out.

I didn’t leave immediately. Instead, I transitioned into server-side development, learning new technologies like Go, AWS, and distributed systems on the fly. It was exciting for a while.

But the same patterns reappeared: complexity without clarity, process without ownership, and a growing sense that the work didn’t matter.

At the same time, something else was happening. AI was accelerating. And I could feel it mattered, more than anything I had worked on before. That feeling was hard to ignore.

So eventually, I left.

For the first time in my career, I didn’t have a clear plan. Just space.

That space gave me time to reflect. And I started to see the pattern clearly: I had spent most of my career looking outward for direction, trusting confident voices, avoiding risk, and not trusting my own instincts.

Underneath it all was fear.

  • Fear of being wrong.
  • Fear of being exposed.
  • Fear of choosing the wrong path.

Meditation helped me see that. And once I could see it, I could start changing it.

Now, I’m approaching my work differently.

I’m not just looking to build software. I want to work on meaningful problems, help people understand complex systems, and operate at the intersection of technology and people, where ideas become real, and clarity matters as much as code.

I don’t have everything figured out. But I trust myself more than I used to.

My Career in Software (So Far)

A story about building things, looking for experts, getting lost in the process, and slowly learning to trust myself.

For most of my adult life, I worked in software.

If I wanted to make that sound neat and professional, I could summarize it quickly: telecom, then cybersecurity, then startup life, then acquisitions, then enterprise scale, then burnout, then leaving, then trying to figure out what comes next.

That version is technically true. It’s also missing almost everything that mattered.

The real story is less tidy. It includes the parts that don’t fit cleanly into a résumé or a LinkedIn summary — the uncertainty, the self-doubt, the environments that shaped me, the moments where I knew something was off but didn’t trust myself enough to act on it, and the long process of realizing that some of the most important things I learned had very little to do with job titles.

For years, I thought I was building a career in software. I was, of course. But I was also doing something else.

I was learning how I respond to authority. How I deal with uncertainty. How much of my life was shaped by fear of being exposed as not good enough. How often I looked outside myself for direction instead of asking what I actually believed or wanted. And how difficult it can be to recognize your own strengths when they don’t look like the strengths you’ve been taught to value most.

This series is my attempt to tell that story honestly.
