A Short Version of My Career Story

I’ve spent more than 20 years working in software across telecom and cybersecurity, including roles at TELUS, Symantec, Immunet, Sourcefire, and Cisco.

On paper, that sounds straightforward enough. In reality, my career has been shaped as much by how I worked and what I learned along the way as by the titles I held or the technologies I used.

I started at TELUS, where I got my first real exposure to building software in the messiest possible way: quickly, with very little process, and with a strong focus on business outcomes over engineering quality. It was exciting, chaotic, and formative. It also taught me early that speed without structure creates fragile systems, even when the business loves the results.

From there I moved to Symantec, expecting to finally learn how software was “supposed” to be built. What I found instead was that structure and professionalism do not automatically create clarity. I worked on client-side systems and installers, learned a lot about enterprise software development, and started to notice how often large organizations become siloed and how easy it is to own a piece of a system without really understanding the whole thing.

Then I joined Immunet, which was the first place I was truly surrounded by real experts. It was also the first time I was fully immersed in cybersecurity as a domain. I had to learn quickly: not just the product, but the world around it — malware detection, telemetry, performance constraints, security trade-offs, Linux and Unix tooling, and a much more intense style of technical collaboration. That environment stretched me enormously. It also showed me how powerful small, aligned, highly capable teams can be.

As Immunet grew into Sourcefire and later into Cisco, I continued working primarily on the client side of the product, building features, expanding endpoint capabilities, and helping make increasingly complex systems visible and understandable. Over time, I found myself doing more and more work around the technology, not just in it: mentoring, onboarding, interviewing, helping teams align, and simplifying complicated problems so people could move forward together.

Later at Cisco, the original startup culture began to erode under scale, process, and organizational distance. I stepped into increasingly large unofficial leadership roles, especially when key people left and important cross-team work still had to move forward. I also lived through a difficult Agile transition where roles like Scrum Master and Product Owner existed on paper but were never really owned in the way they needed to be.

Eventually, that combination of growing complexity, fading culture, leadership gaps, and burnout pushed me to move from endpoint development into server-side work. That gave me another important period of technical growth, where I learned backend and cloud technologies such as Go, AWS, Lambda, DynamoDB, SQS, API Gateway, S3, CloudWatch, X-Ray, Docker, and the broader mindset of working with APIs, distributed systems, observability, and scale.

After about a year and a half on the server side, I realized something deeper hadn’t changed. The bureaucracy, lack of direction, and distance from meaningful product work were still there. At the same time, AI was accelerating in a way that felt impossible to ignore. I had a strong instinct that it was going to transform how we work and what kinds of problems we can solve, and I didn’t want to stay in a role that felt increasingly disconnected from that future.

So I left.

Since then, I’ve spent a lot of time reflecting on my career, deepening my meditation practice, and thinking more honestly about what kind of work actually fits me. What I understand much more clearly now is that my strengths are not limited to writing code. I’m strongest where technology, systems thinking, communication, and human understanding meet: helping people align, making complex systems understandable, reducing confusion, and contributing to work that actually matters.

That’s what I’m looking for now. Not just another job in software, but work that combines technical depth with meaning, clarity, and real impact.

What I’m Open To

  • software engineering
  • technical leadership
  • product or product-adjacent roles
  • research
  • support
  • internal systems
  • hybrid roles where strong technical understanding and strong people instincts both matter

I’m especially interested in areas where AI can have a positive impact on how people learn, make decisions, solve problems, and navigate complexity.

My Career in Software (So Far)

A story about building things, looking for experts, getting lost in the process, and slowly learning to trust myself.

For most of my adult life, I worked in software.

If I wanted to make that sound neat and professional, I could summarize it quickly: telecom, then cybersecurity, then startup life, then acquisitions, then enterprise scale, then burnout, then leaving, then trying to figure out what comes next.

That version is technically true. It’s also missing almost everything that mattered.

The real story is less tidy. It includes the parts that don’t fit cleanly into a résumé or a LinkedIn summary — the uncertainty, the self-doubt, the environments that shaped me, the moments where I knew something was off but didn’t trust myself enough to act on it, and the long process of realizing that some of the most important things I learned had very little to do with job titles.

For years, I thought I was building a career in software. I was, of course. But I was also doing something else.

I was learning how I respond to authority. How I deal with uncertainty. How much of my life was shaped by fear of being exposed as not good enough. How often I looked outside myself for direction instead of asking what I actually believed or wanted. And how difficult it can be to recognize your own strengths when they don’t look like the strengths you’ve been taught to value most.

This series is my attempt to tell that story honestly.
